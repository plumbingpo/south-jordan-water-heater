Keeping Your West Valley City Water Heater Running Longer

Many early failures trace back to maintenance that never happened, and the habits that prevent them are easy. Drain and flush the tank every year to clear sediment, get the anode rod checked every few years, and operate the relief valve annually so it doesn't seize. Set the thermostat close to 120 degrees to reduce scald risk and slow scale buildup. For a tankless heater in a hard-water area, plan on descaling once a year to protect the heat exchanger.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters run 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models typically run 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing warning signs like discolored hot water, rumbling during heating, dampness under the tank, or showers that go cold sooner, a planned replacement is almost always cheaper and far less stressful than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

Minor issues don't stay minor for long. Consider a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a short, affordable repair, but once it's gone, the water starts eating the tank itself, and just like that a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to keep our number in their phone and reach out at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves money and spares you a flooded utility room.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

Around West Valley City, water heater trouble tends to come from a handful of causes. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be part of the picture: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Age is the other big factor. Plenty of homes around here still have the original tank, and once a tank is past about ten years, the anode rod has usually been consumed and rust starts working on the steel from the inside. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Code requirements add another layer. Replacing an older heater often means updating the installation along the way: a thermal expansion tank if the plumbing is a closed system, a T&P discharge line that terminates correctly, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Well water brings its own challenges, such as extra sediment, iron staining, and the sulfur odor that occasionally shows up when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. All of these can be fixed, and most cost far less to address early.
